Predicting Longitudinal Changes in Transtibial Residual Limb Morphology from Serial 3D Scans

Elena Seminati, Trishan Dissanayake, Mark Townsend, Jake Willott, Alexander Power
University of Bath, United Kingdom
Working paper, 2026
Paper Video Code

VoluFit uses 3D surface scans to estimate how residual limb geometry may change over time.

Abstract

Residual limbs change over time due to postoperative healing, muscle atrophy, oedema, body-weight fluctuation, activity and ageing. These changes can affect the fit of a prosthetic socket and may lead to discomfort, skin complications and reduced prosthetic use. This pilot study investigates whether longitudinal three-dimensional (3D) residual limb scans can be used to predict future changes in transtibial residual limb morphology.

Serial scans collected over approximately nine months were represented using 100 horizontal disks and 360 radial measurements per disk. A linear-regression model predicted scaling at each of the resulting 36,000 vertices. The selected model used time since amputation and normalised residual limb geometry, achieving a mean root mean squared error (RMSE) of 3.66 mm during cross-validation and 2.65 mm on two independent test participants. These preliminary results demonstrate feasibility, but require validation in a substantially larger and more diverse cohort before clinical use.

Method

Twenty-one participants were recruited through the NHS Bristol Centre for Enablement. Following withdrawals and exclusions, the final analysis included 12 transtibial participants. Participants were invited to attend nine sessions, approximately four weeks apart, with the first session taking place 6–17 weeks after amputation. At each session the residual limb was scanned in triplicate using an Artec Eva structured-light scanner.

Model development

Ten participants were assigned to training and validation, with two held out for independent testing. A leave-two-out cross-validation procedure generated 45 models for each predictor configuration. Five configurations were compared: no additional participant feature, age, height, estimated tibial length, and normalised residual limb mass. Each configuration also used days after amputation and normalised angle length.

Results

The model with no additional participant feature produced the lowest and most consistent validation error. Although normalised limb mass produced a marginally lower test RMSE, the simpler model was selected because of its stronger validation performance and comparable independent test result.

Clinical relevance

A validated model of future residual limb morphology could support proactive rather than reactive prosthetic management. Predicted changes may eventually help indicate when reassessment or socket modification is appropriate, particularly during the early post-amputation period. Visualising expected changes could also support patient education and self-management.

These applications remain prospective. The model is a research proof of concept and should complement, not replace, professional clinical assessment.

Limitations and future work

  • The final dataset contained only 12 participants and the independent test set contained two.
  • The study included transtibial participants only and was predominantly male.
  • Day-zero geometry was estimated because scanning did not begin immediately after amputation.
  • The model represents morphological change through radial scaling and may not capture more complex 3D deformation.

Future work should use larger and more diverse longitudinal datasets, explore accessible smartphone-based scanning, incorporate additional clinical and behavioural factors, and evaluate more flexible modelling approaches.
